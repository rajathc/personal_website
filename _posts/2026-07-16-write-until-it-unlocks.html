Back in 2016, I decided to take up DE 413 as an elective when I was in IIT Bombay, and it happens to be one of the best and most cornerstone decisions of my life. The course was Introduction to Scriptwriting by Prof. Alka Hingorani, and the wisest and most life-changing advice I have ever received came from that classroom: writing is a craft, and like any craft it can be honed with practice.

Back then Prof. Alka gave us an example from her time studying at Sir JJ College of Architecture, Mumbai, where she photographed the same subject for a hundred days. Something in the act of repetition helps you see the details you have been ignoring, or look deeper into them.

I nodded along in class. It didn't resonate with me until 2018.

NaNoWriMo 2018

The math for NaNoWriMo is simple: 50,000 words in November, which is about 1,667 words per day to stay on track. If you fall behind, the daily target only keeps growing.

Previously I had decided to participate in Inktober and other art challenges, but like all challenges I had dropped them midway as they got too overwhelming. This time, you can say I was more dedicated with following through.

It was gruelling for almost the first 10 days, and it got worse over the next 5. I did average for the first few days, the next 8 days were in the range of 1,300 to 1,500 words, and from day 11 onward it dropped below 1,000 words a day, going as low as 600 words on days 14 and 15. Needless to say, I was struggling, and not just struggling, I was falling behind fast. The required words per day had crossed 2,000, and that number added to the pressure every time I opened the document.

But days 16 and 17 got me back into the 1,600s for the first time since day 2, and something clicked. I stopped caring about perfection and began writing freely, almost like a stream of conscious thought. My relationship with perfection was about to end with writing, and it did end. From then on it went the way Prof. Alka had predicted. I managed 3,000 words a day over the weekend to reduce the backlog, and more than anything, I was finally comfortable with writing. I completed the challenge. Whether those 50,000 words became a book is a secondary question, the draft stays on my hard drive, hidden from all eyes, and I have made peace with it. It unlocked the writing itself.

I have since tried the same challenge with daily sketching as well as journalling, and managed to continue each for more than a year straight. The result was the same every time. Once I stopped counting quality and just kept showing up, it made me free.

Writing is how you find out what you think

It might sound like NaNoWriMo taught me the opposite of thinking, stream of consciousness, just hit the count. And honestly, that November did not produce a single well-examined thought. It just removed the blockage. You cannot examine a thought that never makes it out of your head, and perfectionism keeps most of them in there. A month of bad words got mine out. The examining came later, with the journals.

There has been a lot of talk about AI eroding critical thinking, especially in students. I don't think it is wrong, but I think we blame the wrong part. What we did earlier in schools and colleges, writing assignments and even something as random as a book review, was never really about the assignment. They were reps. I am not saying we all did them honestly, a lot of us managed to pass off our opinion by reading a Wikipedia article or some editorial we found. But the few times we actually wrote it ourselves, we made our critical thinking muscles stronger.

Reading can trick you into feeling like you have an opinion about something. Writing is where I find out whether I actually do. That is what gets lost when the first draft is one prompt away. The essay still gets written, but nobody finds out what they think.

I should be honest about where I sit in all this. I build AI for a living. Our device records your conversations and summarises your day, and I have already written about when summaries help and when they quietly harm. I use these tools every day and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But the more of my reading and remembering I let software compress, the more deliberately I protect the first draft of what I think. I use AI to argue with my paragraph after I have written it. I just never let it go first.

Willful listening

So recently I started journalling about my music listening, in addition to the daily journalling that I do. Every song I sit with gets a few honest lines: what I liked, what I didn't, and how it sounded on my setup. This makes me sit down and dedicate time to listen with intent. It is not analytical listening, it is more of a willful listening. It has helped me be more present, even in conversations, and it is slowly helping me understand what my taste in music actually is, rather than what I assumed it was. You can see the whole journal here.

About a week into the journal, I sat down with Bon Iver's Blood Bank EP and logged it song by song. Beach Baby ends with a minute of instrumental that I have loved for years, and in all these years I never once asked myself what instrument it was. I always thought it was a piano. At some point I guessed it was an electric guitar with distortion and moved on. But when I sat down to write the entry, "nice instrumental at the end" was not going to cut it, so I finally went looking. It is a pedal steel guitar. I had heard that sound a hundred times, and I only actually listened to it the day I had to write about it. Nothing in my life changes because I can name the instrument, but writing is the reason I finally heard it. It keeps doing that with every song.

This works for anything you consume, and you too can pick this up.

The commonplace book

Another routine that I have started following, call it a fad if you want, is the commonplace book. It is a physical notebook that lives in my living room, and my thoughts on the books I am reading go into it, in my own hand and my own words. The rule I follow is simple: my opinion of the book goes down in ink before I read a single review of it. One of the most remarkable articles I read this year was You share other people's thoughts, and it puts it plainly: most of what we pass around as opinion is repackaged from the same few sources, and everything is drifting towards a monocultural way of thinking. The reviews are all out there waiting, and they are usually sharper than mine. But if I read them first, their take quietly becomes my take, and I never find out what I actually thought of the book.

More than anything, the notebook has taught me to choose. Not every thought from a book deserves ink, so each entry forces me to decide what is worth writing, what I can ignore, and what I can think about again and again. The pages I go back to give me a little more each time, which is what Prof. Alka was telling us with her hundred photographs. Somewhere on the internet a feed makes these choices for me. In the notebook I have to make them myself.

Get the draft done

If I had to keep just one thing from all of this, it is the simplest one: get the draft done first, and think about everything else after. The structure, the title, whether any of it is good, all of that can wait. There is nothing to fix until something bad exists.

The other trap is the system. It is very easy to spend weeks picking the right notebook, the right app, the right time of day, a method with a name, and the system slowly becomes the hobby while the writing waits. I have gone down that rabbit hole too. None of it matters. Start writing with whatever is in front of you, and the clarity you were building the system for shows up later, somewhere in the middle of a bad paragraph. It did for me on day 16 of that November.

I should also admit that I wrote half of this post, got stuck, and spent my time worrying about everything else instead. A post about finishing drafts, stuck at the draft stage. So no, I am not preaching, I clearly have not figured this out myself. But I can try, and the trying is the whole exercise. For me, the current version of trying is five sentences about a song. Yours can start anywhere that small.

Closing thoughts

It has been almost ten years since that classroom in IIT Bombay, and Prof. Alka's advice has quietly become how I go about most things I care about: writing, sketching, journalling, and even listening to music. Writing is a craft, and it gets better with practice, just like she said. Doing the reps is how I make sure my thoughts stay mine, and that feels worth protecting right now.
